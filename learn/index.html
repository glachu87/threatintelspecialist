Five habits that prevent most harm

  1. Turn on automatic updates for your phone, computer, browser and router.
  2. Use a password manager so every account has a different password.
  3. Turn on two-step sign-in, starting with your email account.
  4. Slow down on urgent messages. Check requests for money, codes or passwords through a channel you already trust.
  5. Back up what you cannot replace to a second place, such as a cloud service or an external drive.

Glossary

APT
Advanced persistent threat: a well-resourced group, usually linked to a government, that stays hidden in a network for months or years.
Backdoor
A hidden way into a system that skips the normal sign-in, left by an attacker or planted in software.
Botnet
A network of hijacked devices, often home routers and cameras, controlled together by an attacker.
Credential stuffing
Trying usernames and passwords stolen from one site on many others. It works when people reuse passwords.
CVE
A catalogue number for one specific vulnerability, such as CVE-2021-44228, so everyone can refer to the same flaw.
CVSS
A score from 0 to 10 for how severe a vulnerability could be. 9.0 and above is critical. It does not say whether anyone is attacking it.
Data breach
An incident where someone gains access to information they are not allowed to see, such as customer records.
DDoS
Distributed denial of service: flooding a website or service with traffic, often from a botnet, so real users cannot reach it.
EPSS
A daily estimate of how likely a vulnerability is to be attacked in the next 30 days, shown as a percentage.
Exploit
The technique or code that takes advantage of a vulnerability. A vulnerability “being exploited” means attackers are using it for real.
Indicator of compromise (IoC)
A clue that a system has been attacked, such as a known malicious file or internet address. Defenders share these to check their own systems.
Infostealer
Malware that quietly collects saved passwords, browser sessions and payment details from an infected device.
KEV
Known Exploited Vulnerabilities: a list kept by the US agency CISA of flaws with reliable evidence of real attacks.
Leak site
A website where a ransomware group names its victims and publishes stolen data to pressure them. Listings there are claims, not proof.
Malware
Any software written to cause harm: viruses, ransomware, spyware and so on.
OT and ICS
Operational technology and industrial control systems: the computers that run physical equipment such as power grids, water plants and factories.
Passkey
A replacement for passwords that uses your device's fingerprint, face or PIN. It cannot be phished or reused on another site.
Patch
An update that fixes a vulnerability. Installing patches promptly is the single most effective defence most people have.
Phishing
Messages that pretend to be from someone you trust to get you to hand over a password, a code or money.
Ransomware
Malicious software that locks an organisation's files, usually after copying them, so criminals can demand payment.
Social engineering
Tricking a person, rather than a computer, into giving access. Phishing and fake helpdesk calls are examples.
Supply chain attack
Breaking into one company, often a software or service supplier, to reach its customers.
Threat actor
The person or group behind an attack: criminals, state agencies, activists or insiders.
Two-step sign-in (MFA, 2FA)
Signing in with something more than a password, such as a code from an app. It stops most attacks that rely on stolen passwords.
Vulnerability
A mistake in software or hardware that an attacker can use to do something they should not be able to do.
Zero-day
A vulnerability attackers are using before the maker has released a fix. Defenders have had zero days to prepare.
