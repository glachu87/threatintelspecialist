title: A company you use was breached. What to do next

summary: A short checklist for the days after a breach notice, in order of what protects you most.

---

A breach notice is unsettling, and most of them are vague. These steps are in rough order of value. The first three take about fifteen minutes.

## 1. Check that the notice is real

Criminals send fake breach notices to trick people into handing over passwords. Do not click links in the message. Go to the company's website by typing its address yourself, or check the [incident tracker](/incidents/) to see whether the breach is confirmed.

## 2. Change that password, and any copies of it

Change your password for the affected service. If you used the same password anywhere else, change it there too. Attackers routinely try stolen passwords on other sites. A password manager makes unique passwords practical.

## 3. Turn on two-step sign-in

Two-step sign-in (also called MFA or 2FA) means a stolen password is not enough on its own. Turn it on for the affected account and, above all, for your email, because email can reset every other account. An authenticator app or a passkey is stronger than text messages, but text messages are much better than nothing.

## 4. Work out what was exposed

The notice should say what data was involved. What to do depends on the type:

- **Email address or phone number.** Expect more phishing. Be suspicious of messages that mention the breached company.
- **Payment card.** Ask your bank for a replacement card and check recent transactions.
- **Bank details.** Tell your bank and watch for payments you did not set up.
- **Government ID numbers.** Consider a credit freeze or fraud alert with the credit reference agencies in your country.
- **Health or other sensitive records.** Keep the notice. You may be entitled to support or compensation.

## 5. Watch for follow-up scams

After a well-publicised breach, scammers pose as the company, a bank or a "recovery" service. A real organisation will not ask for your password, a one-time code, or payment to protect your account.

## 6. Keep the paperwork

Save the notice and note the date. If the company offers free credit monitoring, it is usually worth accepting. Sign up through the company's own website.

## What not to do

Do not pay anyone who contacts you claiming to hold your data. Do not search for the leaked data yourself: sites that offer it are often traps.
